## 3. Business model: REP, battery ownership, what members pay, how Base earns

### Takeaway
Base is a licensed retail electricity provider: a REP in ERCOT, an ARES in Illinois. It **owns, installs and operates** a large battery at the member's home. The member pays a one-time install fee, sometimes a small monthly membership, and a fixed energy rate. Base earns the retail margin plus wholesale arbitrage, ancillary services, hedging, and fixed capacity (tolling) payments from utilities. The model is a "gentailer": a retailer that also owns generation-like storage, so its long-volatility battery position hedges its short-volatility retail position. That is what "a power company, not a battery company" means.

The parent is Base Power, Inc. — [Base site footer (blog page)](https://www.basepowercompany.com/blog/base-battery-guide); [agents.md](https://www.basepowercompany.com/agents.md)

- Base keeps a **20% state-of-charge reserve** even while grid-balancing. It says this protects members through **97% of Texas outages**, since 97% last under 2.5 hours. **How Base earns**
- Revenue streams: monthly member fees, retail electricity sales, battery arbitrage, and grid-services (capacity) revenue in ERCOT. Example: the Austin Energy **battery tolling agreement**, a fixed payment per kW-month for up to 40 MW, reported as about **$4.08M/yr** and about $40.8M over 10 years (UNVERIFIED; from Sacra and a search summary of City of Austin documents) — [Sacra](https://sacra.com/c/base-power/); [City of Austin RCA](https://services.austintexas.gov/edims/document.cfm?id=471637)

### Inferences
- "Power company, not battery company" in practice: the battery is Base's generation and hedging asset, sited for free at a member's home, and the monthly relationship is a retail electricity contract. Base's value comes from dispatch quality and availability across thousands of assets, not from hardware margin. An orchestration demo should therefore show $ and MW kept available when components fail, not only energy delivered.
- Base's own operating constraints are public: a 20% SoC floor, storm pre-charge, outage prediction, 5-minute SCED base points, load-zone settlement, and utility-dispatched sub-fleets. A simulator that encodes these will sound like Base to Base engineers.

## 4. Scale: members, batteries, MW/MWh, growth rate

### Takeaway
As of Aug 2026, Base had batteries at **about 17,000 homes**, with **more than 23,000 batteries**, **over 500 MWh**, and about **205 MW of nameplate discharge** in its self-operated ERCOT fleet (excluding utility-partner fleets). It installs **about 100 batteries a day, roughly 2 MW or 8 MWh a day**, and aims to double that pace by year-end 2026. Its retail business counts **"30,000+" homeowners or Texans**, which includes energy-only customers. The "~17,000 batteries" the team heard is really **17,000 homes**. The battery count was over 23,000 in early August and is likely higher now.

## 9. Strategy and public statements: the problem Base says it solves

### Takeaway
Base describes itself as "America's next-generation power company". The 2026 message has shifted toward **capacity for load growth and data centers**, **transmission-constraint relief**, and even **distributed compute** on its own fleet.
