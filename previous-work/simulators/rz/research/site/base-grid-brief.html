The gap worth building on is the feeder, not backup

Corrected 26 Sep after design review

Two figures below were revised: SMART-DS transformers are already at standard 25/50/75 kVA nameplate (do not de-rate them), and the 1,000-battery hijack's effect on Texas frequency is a 3–17 mHz band rather than 3–5 mHz. Details are in design/round1/critique-judge.md.

The finding that matters most

ERCOT dispatches and pays Base by load zone. Its rulebook for aggregated home batteries says distribution limits "will not explicitly be enforced" when it dispatches them. Nothing in the market protects a neighbourhood feeder or a 25 kVA street transformer from thousands of batteries that all start charging at once. That is the "dynamic charge/discharge interplay" Base's engineer pointed us to, and a feeder-aware orchestrator is what fills it.

The physics backs this up. A hijacked slice of 1,000 batteries swings about 40 MW. That moves Texas-wide frequency by roughly 3 to 17 thousandths of a hertz, no more than the grid's normal second-to-second wander, yet it is more power than a whole neighbourhood feeder carries.

Almost every input we need is public, and most needs no key:

  • ERCOT's live dashboard feeds: frequency every 10 seconds, prices every 15 minutes, grid condition, fuel mix, grid-battery output.
  • ERCOT's archives, with prices back to 2010. Enough to replay Winter Storm Uri or the September 2023 emergency.
  • ERCOT's own monthly reports on the aggregated home-battery pilot.
  • NREL's synthetic feeders, wired to real north-Austin buildings. OpenDSS solves a 6,800-home substation in 41 ms.

How the money works

Texas splits electricity in two. A regulated wires company (a TDSP, such as Oncor or CenterPoint) delivers the power. A competitive Retail Electric Provider (REP) sells it. Base is a licensed REP (PUCT #10338) that also owns, installs, maintains and remotely operates a large battery at each member's home.

  • Member pays: a one-time install fee of $95–$695 (typically $695 in Oncor territory), a $19 monthly membership, and electricity on a 36-month fixed plan of 8¢/kWh plus delivery, about 14.3¢/kWh all-in on Oncor at 2,000 kWh a month (27 Aug 2026). (Base energy page, Electrek)
  • Base earns from four streams: retail margin, wholesale energy arbitrage, ancillary-service payments, and fixed capacity (tolling) payments from utilities. [Base's CEO] describes the two books as a natural hedge: price volatility hurts a fixed-rate retailer and pays a battery owner. (Latitude Media)

That is what "a power company, not a battery company" means: the battery is generation and hedging capacity, sited for free at the customer's meter, and the product sold is electricity plus backup. No primary source splits revenue by stream. Sacra's estimates of about $12M for 2025 and $70M projected for 2026 are UNVERIFIED.

3 · Product

Each battery is a 20 kW grid asset that must never strand its home

Operating rules precise enough to code

  • Base controls the battery, not the member. Base calls it a grid-balancing battery, not a self-consumption system.
  • 20% reserve at all times. Base says this covers 97% of Texas outages (under 2.5 hours), and that batteries rarely drop below 50% in practice. The contract only promises to "endeavor" to hold it.
  • Utility programs may dispatch 80% of capacity; 20% stays for backup.
  • Storm mode: when long outages are likely, the battery holds more charge and pulls back from grid activity. Target level and lead time are unpublished.
  • Outages: the system powers the home alone and never discharges to the grid. Backup only starts if the home draws less than the start limit; after an overload it retries three times, then needs a manual restart.
  • Normal use: grid support mostly mid-to-late afternoon on hot days; dispatchable up to 500 cycles a year.
  • Backup duration (one Core): 22–36 h at 750 W, 4–6 h at 4 kW, 2–3 h at 8 kW.

Member's reserve is a hard limit

The Texas market in plain terms

ERCOT runs a market that pays only for energy and reserves, not for capacity. Every five minutes its Security-Constrained Economic Dispatch (SCED) picks the cheapest mix of generators and batteries that meets demand without overloading transmission lines, and sets a price at each location (the LMP). Retail load settles on 15-minute prices averaged over load zones.

Ancillary services are paid standby jobs:

  • Regulation adjusts output every four seconds.
  • Responsive Reserve (RRS) catches generator trips. Its Fast Frequency Response slice must deliver fully within 0.25 s once frequency hits 59.85 Hz.
  • ECRS must respond within 10 minutes; Non-Spin within 30.

On 5 Dec 2025 ERCOT switched to RTC+B (Real-Time Co-optimization plus Batteries): SCED buys reserves alongside energy every five minutes; each battery is modeled as one device with a state of charge; the old ORDC scarcity adder on energy prices is gone (scarcity now shows up in reserve prices); the real-time offer cap is $2,000/MWh, with $5,000/MWh for day-ahead and the system price ceiling. Batteries must hold enough energy behind each reserve award: 30 min for Regulation and RRS, 1 h for ECRS, 4 h for Non-Spin. (ERCOT, Modo Energy)

Transmission costs are allocated by 4CP: each customer's demand in the single highest 15-minute interval of June, July, August and September. That's why co-ops pay to shave those peaks.

How Base's fleet plugs in

Base reaches the wholesale market through ERCOT's ADER pilot (Aggregate Distributed Energy Resource), which lets many metered homes in one load zone and one distribution utility bid as a single resource. Each home must be ≤1 MW and the aggregation ≥100 kW; telemetry goes to ERCOT every two seconds. The distribution utility can reject homes at registration for safety or reliability, but after that, nothing in ERCOT's dispatch knows which feeder or transformer a battery sits on. (ADER Governing Document 3.3)

  • Base forms one ADER per load zone, bids it into SCED every five minutes, settles at the load-zone price, and carries Non-Spin and ECRS.
  • Jul–Aug 2026 partitions: LZ_NORTH 22.9 MW, LZ_SOUTH 7.2 MW, LZ_HOUSTON 50.5 MW, 30 MW pending. That's 39% of the 205.5 MW fleet.
  • About 60 days from install to SCED participation, paced by manual registration and ERCOT's network-model releases.
  • ERCOT raised the pilot cap to 500 MW in March 2026. ECRS hit its 100 MW cap by June, so new ADER megawatts can only earn energy and Non-Spin for now. LZ_AEN carried zero ADER MW.
  • Market share doesn't reconcile: Base claims 103 of 145 MW (71%); ERCOT's June tracker shows 248.7 MW approved and its August workbook 292.9 MW across nine aggregations. Use ERCOT for totals, Base for Base.
Base's published tracking record

Houston partition, evening of 22 Jul 2026 (ERCOT's all-time demand record): 46.9 MW of discharge capability; 36 of 36 five-minute intervals inside ERCOT's tolerance (the greater of 2 MW or 15%); mean miss 1.59 MW (about 3.3%); worst miss 5.45 MW during a ramp from 12.1 to 46.7 MW in 15 minutes. Location is becoming the product

On 16 Jan 2026 Base's hourly charge and discharge blocks moved ERCOT's estimated flow at two 138 kV buses in the expected direction on all ten transitions, by 1.4–1.7 MW per MW cycled, a greater-than-one ratio Base calls unexplained. Its siting math: 40 MW placed in an import pocket (PTDF 0.83–0.88 on the constrained line) gives as much relief as about 490 MW spread across the zone. Base backs a proposed ADER Phase IV that would recognize aggregations by transmission node.

The economics point the same way. Batteries already supply most of ERCOT's reserves (2025: 94% of Reg-Up, 51% of RRS, 42% of ECRS). Revenue per MW of storage fell about 37% in 2025 as the statewide fleet passed 17 GW. In the record week of July 2026, batteries discharged a record 11,980 MW, yet real-time prices peaked at only $378/MWh. (Potomac Economics, Grid Status) Arbitrage alone is a shrinking prize; firm, locatable capacity that keeps working when parts fail is not.

For scale, grid-scale ERCOT storage earned about $28,800/MW-year: roughly $1.58 a day for a 20 kW Core and $0.90 for an 11.4 kW legacy unit DERIVED, a benchmark rather than Base's revenue.

5 · Physics

Frequency is one number for Texas; voltage is a street-by-street problem

Grid physics without the jargon

Picture every large generator in Texas pedalling one enormous tandem bicycle at 60 turns a second. If riders drop off (a generator trips) or the load gets heavier, the bike slows and frequency falls. Three things then happen in order:

  1. Spinning mass (inertia) buys a few seconds.
  2. Automatic governors push harder in proportion to the slowdown (droop).
  3. If frequency keeps falling, relays disconnect customers (under-frequency load shedding).

ERCOT plans for losing 2,750 MW at once and keeps a critical inertia of about 100 GW·s. Real events: the 2022 Odessa disturbance lost 2,555 MW and pulled frequency to 59.7 Hz; on 15 Feb 2021 Winter Storm Uri drove it to 59.302 Hz and held it below 59.4 Hz for 4 min 23 s, against a 9-minute limit before more generators would trip. Shedding stages: 5% of load at 59.3 Hz, 15% cumulative at 58.9 Hz, 25% at 58.5 Hz (a 2025 notice adds 59.1 and 58.7 Hz without clear percentages). The IEEE 1547 default for home inverters is 5% droop with a 0.036 Hz deadband. (NERC Odessa, ERCOT Uri review)

The rule that shapes everything else

Frequency is the same everywhere on the Texas grid. Voltage is local, sagging along each street as current flows. A fleet's effect on frequency is counted in system-wide megawatts; its voltage and overheating effects are counted feeder by feeder.

Distribution is where the fleet lives. A substation steps transmission voltage down. Each feeder is one medium-voltage circuit, commonly 12.47 kV, leaving the substation through its own breaker to serve a neighbourhood. Service transformers on poles or pads step 7.2 kV down to 120/240 V for a few homes. NREL's synthetic Austin feeders have medians of about 660 customers, a 6.9 MW peak and 2.5 homes per transformer.

  • Customer voltage must stay within 114–126 V (ANSI Range A).
  • Transformer insulation ages about twice as fast for every 6 °C its hot spot runs above rating.
  • A worked example of an 11 MW feeder could accept only 0.17–3.3 MW pushed back from homes, depending on location; voltage usually bound first.

Each additional battery, with Base's real numbers DERIVED

  • Feeder: an 11 MW feeder at 90% loading has 1.1 MW of room. 56 Cores (or 97 legacy units) charging at once use it up; each extra Core adds about 0.18 points of loading.
  • Transformer: one Core charging flat out is about 80% of a 25 kVA transformer by itself. With 4–6 kW of evening load on each of 2.5 homes, one charging Core takes a 25 kVA unit to about 123–144%: past its 110% normal limit but under its 150% emergency rating, so it ages the transformer rather than blowing a fuse. Only about a quarter of SMART-DS transformers are 25 kVA; a 50 kVA unit in the same case sits near 70%.
  • Concentration: penetration grows 1–2% of homes a year, but Lennar-style subdivisions put a battery on nearly every home, and Base's zone-wide charging blocks (−45.8 MW in Houston inside 15 minutes) land on whichever feeders host the batteries.

A feeder-aware controller computes headroom per transformer and feeder from forecast load, staggers charging after a price drop, and discharges first on overloaded feeders.

A 1,000-battery hijack is a neighbourhood emergency, not a Texas one

A battery is a better weapon than an air conditioner because it swings both ways. A Core flipping from full charge to full discharge moves 40 kW, so 1,000 Cores swing 40 MW (1,000 legacy units about 23 MW). Two public data points give a rough rate: Odessa moved frequency about 0.12 mHz per MW; ERCOT's 7 Aug 2026 event about 0.075 mHz per MW. So 40 MW moves Texas frequency by roughly 3–17 mHz DERIVED, depending on load damping and deadband assumptions. Normal ERCOT wander on 25 Sep 2026 had a standard deviation of about 13.7 mHz, so either end is lost in the noise. Quote it as a band, never a single number.

On feeders the same attack is catastrophic: spread across three median Austin feeders it is about 6.7 MW each, roughly each feeder's entire peak. That opens breakers, pushes voltage outside 114–126 V, and overloads every transformer with a battery on it. Princeton's BlackIoT study agrees from the other side: frequency attacks need 200–300 compromised devices per MW of demand, line-overload attacks only 4–10. (BlackIoT, USENIX 2018)

Only at whole-fleet scale does it touch frequency: swinging all 205.5 MW moves about 411 MW (15% of the design trip, roughly 30–50 mHz); adding the ~190 MW of utility programs nears 800 MW DERIVED. So the metric that matters is blast radius per credential: how many megawatts one compromised service can command, against a feeder's capacity and 1% of system load.

Detection has to rely on physics, not command logs

A backdoor radio or a stolen credential bypasses the fleet's own command log, so detectors compare what devices claim with what physics shows. These are design recommendations, not documented Base practice.

  • Physics checks: battery-reported power vs the utility meter's net change; fleet total plus baseline vs the feeder-head measurement; transformer voltage should fall when a battery claims to charge.
  • Drift detection: stealth attacks stay under alarms (SoC reported 3% high, 80% of an award delivered, 2 s of added lag). CUSUM or EWMA against peers on the same feeder, firmware and install batch beats fixed thresholds.
  • Bounded commands: signed commands with sequence numbers and expiry; ramp limits on the device; at most one charge/discharge flip every few minutes; a random 0–120 s start delay; control shards capped below feeder hosting limits.
  • Local autonomy: IEEE 1547 frequency-watt and volt-var reflexes, a hard SoC floor, refuse to charge at low frequency or export at high voltage.
  • Tampering signals: never exports during events, SoC pinned at 100%, "offline" exactly during dispatch windows, meter sees no export when the battery claims it.

What this means for our build

The market sees one resource per load zone. Physics sees thousands of 20 kW loads, each on a specific transformer. The most credible thing our simulator can produce is a number nobody publishes: how many batteries a real-looking Austin feeder can host before the first violation, under naive versus feeder-aware dispatch, and how many megawatts of market commitment survive when pieces fail.

Track fit

  • Orchestration (primary). Coordinating many independent things and holding up when pieces fail is exactly Base's operating problem, and the feeder gap gives the "problem" and "why" lines (30 points) a justification Base itself has written down.
  • Open Grid Data (second). The simulator runs on live and historical ERCOT feeds, ADER workbooks and frequency-event data, and can surface a data insight, e.g. how often real load-zone price drops would have triggered simultaneous charging on a feeder.
  • Most Commercializable is the alternative second track if we lead with dollars; the value metrics below can carry that argument inside Orchestration either way.
  • Label the grid honestly: SMART-DS north-Austin feeders are either an Austin Energy-dispatched sub-fleet priced at LZ_AEN, or a stand-in for an Oncor suburb on Base's ERCOT path. Say which.

Questions to ask Base engineers on site

  1. When a unit loses connectivity, does it hold its last set point, ramp to zero after a timeout, or run a local schedule? After how long?
  2. Do you enforce feeder or transformer limits in dispatch today? What do the TDSPs give you: feeder IDs, transformer mapping, hosting limits?
  3. How is a partition's base point split across devices: by headroom, by SoC, with random delay?
  4. What do the ~61% of self-operated MW outside ADER do?
  5. Which load zones do Oncor Austin-suburb members settle in? Do Austin Energy tolling batteries ever take ERCOT dispatch?
  6. When Austin Energy or CoServ and the Markets desk want opposite things, who wins and how?
  7. What are the storm-mode charge target and lead time, the reconnect delay, and the recharge staggering after restoration?
  8. What are the Core's usable kWh, round-trip efficiency and surge rating?
  9. How did the fleet perform during Winter Storm Fern (Jan 2026) and on 22 Jul 2026?
  10. How do you detect tampering, or telemetry that disagrees with the meter?
  11. What explains the greater-than-one flow change per MW at the 138 kV buses?
  12. Do TDSPs upgrade service transformers when a Core is installed?
